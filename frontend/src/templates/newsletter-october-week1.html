📰 Cybersecurity News of the Week

Headline: Fake Mobile Networks Could Intercept Your Bank OTP — NCC-CSIRT Issues Advisory

Summary: The OSGF (Office of the Secretary to the Government of the Federation) alerted Nigerian authorities about a cybercrime case in the UAE involving a criminal group that hijacked mobile signals and conducted SMS-based banking fraud. The group used specialized equipment to create fake mobile networks, intercept SMS messages, and send fraudulent messages impersonating banks to steal financial information. This technique poses a potential risk to Nigeria's telecom infrastructure and financial systems due to the country's large subscriber base and reliance on SMS banking.

Source: Magnific

📌 Key Details:

  • Impact: The attack technique uses rogue cellular infrastructure designed to mimic legitimate mobile networks. The criminals deploy signal-jamming equipment to temporarily disrupt legitimate cellular signals within a targeted area. Once legitimate connectivity is weakened, the attackers activate a rogue base station (fake cellular tower) that broadcasts a stronger signal, causing nearby mobile devices to automatically connect to the attacker-controlled network. After devices connect, attackers can send spoofed SMS messages appearing to originate from legitimate financial institutions, intercept SMS communications including one-time passwords (OTPs), and conduct large-scale smishing campaigns targeting banking customers. The advisory lists the following impacts: unauthorized access to bank accounts through stolen credentials or OTP interception; large-scale financial fraud targeting mobile banking users; manipulation of SMS communications used for transaction authentication; loss of customer trust in telecom and banking systems; and possible use of rogue networks for surveillance or data interception.
  • Affected: GSM and LTE mobile networks; SMS messaging infrastructure; mobile banking systems using SMS authentication; telecommunications spectrum environments; and mobile subscribers within proximity of a rogue base station. Platform: Telecommunication and Mobile Ecosystems. Versions: all types and versions.
  • Mitigation: NCC-CSIRT recommends the following steps: deploy rogue base station detection systems across network infrastructure; strengthen radio spectrum monitoring to detect abnormal signal activity; implement mechanisms to detect and block unauthorized BTS transmissions; collaborate with security agencies to track illegal telecom equipment; and enhance monitoring of SMS gateways and messaging platforms.

⚠️ Why It Matters: The advisory notes that this technique is particularly dangerous because it operates at the network layer of the telecommunications network, allowing attackers to bypass traditional Internet security controls. Given Nigeria's large subscriber base and reliance on SMS banking, NCC-CSIRT identifies it as a potential risk to the country's telecom infrastructure and financial systems, and rates the damage as Critical and the probability as High.

🔗 Source: NCC-CSIRT Advisory

🎯 Threat of the Week (Sector-by-Sector)

  • Technology & Software Development (macOS Users, Developers and AI Tool Users): ngCERT is aware of an ongoing ClickFix (pastejacking) campaign targeting developers and users of AI tools. The campaign distributes Atomic macOS Stealer (AMOS), a sophisticated information-stealing malware affecting macOS systems. The attack leverages social engineering techniques to trick users into executing malicious terminal commands. ClickFix (pastejacking) is a social engineering technique that manipulates users into copying and executing malicious commands from deceptive sources such as fake documentation pages, malicious advertisements, or AI-generated content. In this campaign, attackers disguise harmful terminal commands, often obfuscating them with encoding techniques such as base64, as legitimate setup or troubleshooting instructions. Once executed, these commands download and install AMOS. The malware establishes persistence on the compromised system, harvests sensitive information (including credentials and files), and may deploy additional backdoors to enable persistent remote access.
    Read full analysis →

⚠️ Why It Matters: If successfully exploited, this campaign may result in: credential theft, including browser-stored passwords and Apple Keychain data; unauthorized remote access through backdoor mechanisms; data exfiltration, including sensitive files and developer assets; compromise of cryptocurrency wallets and financial information; deployment of additional malware and further system exploitation; and organisational risks such as supply chain compromise and credential leakage.

Recommended Mitigation (ngCERT):

  • Avoid executing terminal commands from untrusted sources, including advertisements, unknown websites, or unverified AI-generated content.
  • Verify all documentation and instructions, ensuring they originate from official vendor domains.
  • Educate users and developers on pastejacking techniques and social engineering risks.
  • Deploy Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) solutions to monitor suspicious shell activity.
  • Restrict and monitor script execution on macOS systems.
  • Enable and maintain built-in protections such as Gatekeeper and XProtect.
  • Regularly update macOS and security tools to detect emerging threats.
  • Monitor for unusual outbound network traffic and unauthorized persistence mechanisms.
  • Implement least-privilege access controls to limit the impact of compromised accounts.

🔗 Source: ngCERT Advisory

🛠️ Tool of the Week

Spotlight: Canarytokens by Thinkst (Free Digital Tripwires) — Free to use, no account required

What it does:
Canarytokens (canarytokens.org) is a free service from Thinkst that creates "tripwires" you can plant inside your own systems. A token can be a web link (URL), a hostname, a fake AWS key, or a decoy file such as a Word document, PDF, MySQL dump or kubeconfig. When someone opens or uses it, you receive an alert by email or webhook, so an intruder effectively announces themselves. It lets defenders place traps in real systems instead of setting up separate honeypots.

Who should use it:

  • Cybersecurity Analysts
  • SOC Analysts
  • Incident Responders
  • IT / System Administrators
  • Small and Medium-sized Businesses without a dedicated deception-platform budget

Why it matters:
Attackers who get into a network look for interesting files and credentials. A Canarytoken looks like exactly that, but it has no real value and no legitimate use, so an alert is a strong sign that someone is where they should not be. It needs no infrastructure to deploy, and organisations that prefer to keep token data on their own systems can self-host it.

⚔️ Attack Scenario (Case Study)

Incident: The Fake Login Page That Steals Your "Logged-In" Status, Even With MFA Turned On

Entry Point (How it started):
An accountant at a mid-sized Lagos firm received an email that looked like a shared-document notification from a client. The link opened a page that looked exactly like the company's Microsoft 365 sign-in. The page was actually a proxy run by the attacker. It passed everything the victim typed to the real Microsoft site and passed the real site's replies back to the victim, so the page behaved normally.

What the attacker did next:

  • The accountant entered their email and password. The proxy forwarded them to the real sign-in page.
  • The real site asked for the MFA code. The accountant entered the code from their authenticator app, and the proxy forwarded that too.
  • The real site then logged the user in and issued a session cookie, the small token that keeps you signed in. The proxy copied it.
  • The accountant saw a normal document and suspected nothing.
  • The attacker loaded the stolen cookie into their own browser and was logged in as the accountant. No password or MFA prompt was needed.
  • Inside the mailbox, the attacker created a hidden rule to forward selected emails to an outside address and delete the alerts. They then browsed shared files and client folders.

Final Impact:

  • Client documents and internal files were viewed and copied.
  • The compromise went unnoticed for days because the login looked legitimate and MFA had "passed".
  • Resetting the password alone did not remove the attacker's access until active sessions were revoked.
  • The firm had to notify affected clients and review all mailbox rules and connected apps.

🔑 Key Lesson: MFA is essential, but not all MFA is equal. Codes typed into a page and "Approve" prompts can be relayed by a fake site in real time. Phishing-resistant methods such as passkeys or FIDO2 security keys check the real website's address, so they will not work on a look-alike page. After any suspected phishing click, change the password, revoke all active sessions, and check the mailbox for unfamiliar forwarding rules.

🔗 Source: Microsoft Security Blog (MFA relay phishing guidance)

💼 Career Spotlight

Malware Analyst

A Malware Analyst dissects malicious software to understand how it works, how it spreads, and how to detect or remove it. Using specialised tools and techniques, they break down malware samples, identify their functions and assess the threat they pose. Their findings feed directly into incident response plans, detection rules and security tools that protect organisations, which makes this role central to defending against threats such as the information-stealing malware featured in this week's Threat of the Week.

  • Salary: $96K - $176K / year. Actual pay varies by employer, experience and certifications.
  • Certs needed: GIAC Reverse Engineering Malware (GREM), which covers analysing malicious Office macros and PDFs, obfuscated malware, and unpacking and debugging packed malware (no formal prerequisites). Other credentials commonly listed in employer job postings include GIAC Certified Forensic Analyst (GCFA), GIAC Certified Incident Handler (GCIH) and Certified Reverse Engineering Analyst (CREA).

📖 Cybersecurity Glossary

ClickFix (Pastejacking): A social engineering technique that manipulates users into copying and executing malicious commands from deceptive sources such as fake documentation pages, malicious advertisements, or AI-generated content. The commands are often disguised as legitimate setup or troubleshooting instructions and, once run, can install malware such as information stealers.

Rogue Base Station: A fake cellular tower designed to mimic a legitimate mobile network. It broadcasts a stronger signal so that nearby phones connect to it automatically, allowing attackers to send spoofed SMS messages, intercept SMS communications such as one-time passwords (OTPs), and run large-scale smishing (SMS phishing) campaigns.
